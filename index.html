What a gilt ladder is — and what one costs today (a worked example, not linked to your plan)
What this is. A calculator that prices a maths problem from public data: “how much would it cost, at today’s Bank of England yield curve, to have gilts mature and pay £X every year for N years?” It uses the live list of gilts in issue (Debt Management Office) and delayed exchange prices for illustration.
What it is not. It does not know whether a ladder suits you, which gilt you should buy, or how it interacts with your tax position; it is not a recommendation and not financial advice. A bond ladder is not necessarily the best strategy for anyone — the three strategies above are the honest comparison. Figures ignore dealing costs, bid–offer spreads, accrued interest, whole-unit rounding and the tax treatment of coupons. If in doubt, take regulated advice.
How a ladder works, in four lines
  1. You buy one gilt maturing in each year you want income. When it matures the government repays the face value — that year’s money.
  2. Index-linked gilts repay face value uplifted by inflation, so a real ladder pays a constant amount in today’s money; conventional gilts repay a fixed £ amount.
  3. The cost today of £1 in year k is £1 ÷ (1 + yield)k; the yield curve says what the market charges for each year. Long years are cheap when real yields are high.
  4. Nothing about a gilt ladder is guaranteed except the cash flows if held to maturity: prices move daily, and selling early can lose money.

Further reading: the DMO’s About gilts, Monevator’s Should you build an index-linked gilt ladder?, and LateGenXer’s Gilt Ladder Builder (whose approach this example follows).

Your income shape — the one number every tool works to, year by year

Your budget is what you spend now. Retirement spending usually runs in three phases — go-go (travel, projects, the years you are fit), go-slow (from the mid-70s, spending typically drifts down 1–2% a year in real terms), and no-go (the basics, and care if it comes). Nobody can budget those years line by line, so don't try: start from today's figure, then draw the steps you'd actually want. Above the budget is fine. Below your essentials turns orange.

Household — will the money last for both of you?

Your Stress Tester answers "will my money last?". If you're a couple, that's only half the question: your incomes start in different years, your pensions are different sizes, and you share the spending. This tab joins your two plans together and answers the question neither can answer alone — can we retire, together, and when is the money tight?
The two plans
No plan for them yet?
How it works
  1. Your plan is already set up — budget, pots, State Pension, all of it.
  2. Give your partner a plan of their own. Open the plan menu (▾ at the top), create a plan in their name, and fill in their Stress Tester settings — their pots, their State Pension date, and their share of the household budget (the Budget tool's per-person split gives you each side's number).
  3. Pick their plan above and run the check.

You'll get three things you can't see from either plan alone:

  • One answer: the percentage of 1,000 possible market futures in which the money lasted for both of you — run through the same markets, because a crash hits you both in the same year.
  • One timeline: year by year, what the household needs, when each State Pension starts, and how much has to come out of the pots — including the tight "bridge" years before your State Pensions arrive.
  • One number for what's left: your combined pots over time, in today's money, if markets are poor, typical, or strong.

Why two plans and not one joint plan? UK pensions, ISAs and tax are strictly individual — two personal allowances, two State Pensions, two sets of pots. Keeping a plan each gets the tax right; this tab puts the answers side by side. Your own plan's tabs are unchanged.
